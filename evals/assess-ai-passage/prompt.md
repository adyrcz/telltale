Elara Voss had always believed that bread remembered the hands that made it.

The bakery had been her grandmother's before it was hers, and in the gray hour before dawn, the kitchen held its breath with her. The air smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and something she couldn't name—something like home.

It wasn't the fire that broke her. It was the silence after.

Three weeks ago, the flames had taken the back storeroom, the old recipe box, and the photograph of her grandmother that had hung above the ovens for forty years. Insurance would cover the walls. Nothing would cover the rest.

Her hands trembled as she measured the flour. Her chest ached with a grief she didn't have words for. Outside, the first light crept across the empty square, pale and uncertain, as if the morning itself wasn't sure it should begin.

The bell above the door chimed. Mrs. Kowalczyk stood in the doorway, holding a small tin box.

"Your grandmother gave me this in 1987," she said softly. "She told me to keep it safe until you needed it."

Inside were the recipes—every one, copied in her grandmother's careful hand.

Elara's breath caught. In that moment, she understood: some things can't be destroyed. They live on in the people we love.

And for the first time in weeks, that was enough.
